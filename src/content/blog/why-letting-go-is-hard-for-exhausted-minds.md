I was lying in bed after a brutally hard day, exhausted to my bones but completely wide awake. My mind was running in endless loops. It wasn't just vague, abstract anxiety — I was tangled in very specific, relentless thoughts. Fear for the future. Frustrations from my job. Tension in my personal life.

We all have troubles. We always have thoughts that refuse to quiet down. That is just life. But in that moment, I just needed to sleep.

Desperate, I tried a technique my Kundalini teacher had taught me: imagine your thoughts as clouds in the sky, and watch them peacefully float away.

Sounds beautifully serene, right?

In reality, it was an exhausting struggle. My brain was already in overdrive, and now I was demanding it do even more work. I had to conjure up a mental sky, build a fluffy cloud, somehow attach that lingering work frustration or fear of the future to it, and then force myself to neutrally observe it drifting off.

It didn't feel peaceful. It felt like trying to walk a tightrope in a circus while juggling and playing a musical instrument at the exact same time. The sheer mental effort of visualizing the clouds while simultaneously trying to detach from the looping thoughts was just too much. I couldn't hold the image, and the heavy thoughts kept crashing right back down.

## A Long Tradition of "Letting Go"

After that frustrating night, I needed to know why my brain was fighting this so hard. If this technique was supposed to bring peace, why did it feel like a battle?

It turns out, the concept of observing thoughts as passing objects isn't new — it has just undergone a massive rebrand over the centuries. Buddhist contemplative traditions, including Vipassana and Zen, have long emphasized observing thoughts and experiences without automatically reacting to them. The metaphors vary — sometimes it's clouds in the sky, sometimes leaves floating down a stream — but many of these practices share an emphasis on observing thoughts without automatically becoming caught up in them.

In the 1980s, psychologist Dr. Steven C. Hayes developed Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and looked at this approach through a modern psychological lens. He realized human suffering is deeply tied to language. We treat the sentences in our heads as literal, urgent truths. If the thought "I am going to fail at this" pops up, your brain doesn't process it as a simple string of nouns and verbs. A thought about failure can still trigger a genuine emotional and physical response, making a hypothetical future feel immediate and urgent.

ACT introduced a clinical term for this ancient practice: **Cognitive Defusion.**

Normally, we are "fused" with our thoughts. Defusion is the act of stepping back from your thoughts and changing your relationship with the internal language in your head. You stop treating the thought as a reality that must be solved, and start seeing it for what it actually is: a temporary string of words generated by an overzealous, problem-solving brain.

## From Mental Gymnastics to a Tactile Solution

The idea has deep roots in contemplative practice, and modern psychological approaches have developed their own ways of working with thoughts. But the execution in our modern, overstimulated world is deeply flawed. When you are mentally depleted, forcing your brain to construct a serene landscape and actively deconstruct your own thoughts requires a massive amount of cognitive energy you just don't have.

As a product designer, I spend my days trying to reduce friction in digital spaces. One night, after another failed attempt at visualizing those mental clouds, I had a realization: my inability to let go wasn't a meditation failure. It was a user experience problem. The cognitive load of the exercise was simply too high for an exhausted mind.

If holding the image in my mind's eye was the barrier, the solution was to offload that heavy lifting. I didn't want to force my brain to imagine a sky; I wanted to just look at one.

So, I decided to build a tangible version of the exercise for myself. That experiment eventually became the foundation for Calmisu.

I wanted to take the abstract concept of cognitive defusion and make it tactile. Instead of holding a looping thought in your mind, you type it out. The fear, the tension, or the frustration goes directly into a cloud on the screen. The sky is already there, doing the visual work for you.

You don't have to passively hope the thought disappears. You can watch it drift, and if it feels stubborn, you can physically swipe it away.

Giving my eyes and hands something concrete to do took all the pressure off my imagination. By externalizing the exercise, my mind finally had the space to step back, observe the loop, and just rest — no circus act required.
